Title: COUNSELBENCH: A LARGE-SCALE EXPERT EVALUA-TION AND ADVERSARIAL BENCHMARKING OF LARGE LANGUAGE MODELS IN MENTAL HEALTH QUESTION ANSWERING
Abstract: Medical question answering (QA) benchmarks often focus on multiple-choice or fact-based tasks, leaving open-ended answers to real patient questions underexplored. This gap is particularly critical in mental health, where patient questions often mix symptoms, treatment concerns, and emotional needs, requiring answers that balance clinical caution with contextual sensitivity. We present COUNSEL-BENCH, a large-scale benchmark developed with 100 mental health professionals to evaluate and stress-test large language models (LLMs) in realistic help-seeking scenarios. The first component, COUNSELBENCH-EVAL, contains 2,000 expert evaluations of answers from GPT-4, LLaMA 3, Gemini, and online human therapists on patient questions from the public forum CounselChat. Each answer is rated across six clinically grounded dimensions, with span-level annotations and written rationales. Expert evaluations show that while LLMs achieve high scores on several dimensions, they also exhibit recurring issues, including unconstructive feedback, overgeneralization, and limited personalization or relevance. Responses were frequently flagged for safety risks, most notably unauthorized medical advice. Follow-up experiments show that LLM judges systematically overrate model responses and overlook safety concerns identified by human experts. To probe failure modes more directly, we construct COUNSELBENCH-ADV, an adversarial dataset of 120 expert-authored mental health questions designed to trigger specific model issues. Expert evaluation of 1,080 responses from nine LLMs reveals consistent, model-specific failure patterns. Together, COUNSELBENCH establishes a clinically grounded framework for benchmarking LLMs in mental health QA.

Section: INTRODUCTION
Large language models (LLMs) have shown impressive progress in general-purpose question answering (QA), and there is growing interest in their use for healthcare applications (Thirunavukarasu et al., 2023). However, deployment in high-stakes settings requires rigorous evaluation of both capability and risk (Ali et al., 2023). Existing medical QA benchmarks such as MedQA (Jin et al., 2020) and MedMCQA (Pal et al., 2022) largely emphasize multiple-choice or fact-based tasks (Zhang et al., 2025). While these benchmarks measure factual recall, they fail to capture how models perform on the open-ended questions that patients actually ask. Open-ended QA presents greater challenges: questions are ambiguous, responses cannot be reduced to a single correct answer, and evaluation requires criteria that extend beyond factual accuracy.
These challenges are particularly critical in mental health QA. Here, patient queries are expressed as free-text descriptions of symptoms, treatment concerns, and emotional needs, and helpful responses must balance empathy, actionable guidance, and professional boundaries (Elliott et al., 2011;Na, 2024;Meadi et al., 2025). Unlike other medical domains, where diagnostic tests or structured knowledge often constrain the answer space, mental health QAs are inherently subjective and context-dependent (Association, 2013;Hormazábal-Salgado et al., 2024). Moreover, mental health QA has direct real-world significance: platforms such as CounselChat (Bertagnolli, 2020), peer-support forums (Pendse et al., 2019), EHR-based messaging, and digital mental health services like NOCD (NOCD, 2018) primarily operate through single-turn interactions, with limited followup in most cases. These setting are designed for brief, asynchronous exchanges that offer emotional support, psychoeducation, or coping strategies in a concise format. Such platforms are widely used and often serve as a primary source of support, especially for individuals who may not have access to or feel ready for full-length therapy (Naslund et al., 2016;Merchant et al., 2022). In such settings, even small errors, such as speculative claims, unsafe recommendations, or dismissive tone, can have immediate negative consequences. Evaluating LLMs on authentic, open-ended mental health QA in a standardized framework that enables consistent comparison of model responses to identical patient questions is therefore both technically and socially urgent.
While recent work has begun to explore mental health QA, current resources remain limited. Many benchmarks continue to rely on multiple-choice proxies, which bypass the ambiguity and contextual demands of free-text responses (Racha et al., 2025). Others use small expert panels or LLM-as-judge protocols (Sun et al., 2021;Szymanski et al., 2024;Racha et al., 2025), approaches that provide efficiency but often miss clinically salient failures. These limitations highlight the need for a large-scale, clinician-grounded benchmark for open-ended mental health QA, as well as resources designed to stress-test LLMs and surface failure modes that matter in practice.
We introduce CounselBench to address this gap. Our contributions are threefold:
1. Clinically grounded evaluation dimensions: we define six criteria for open-ended mental health QA, capturing both quality and safety. 2. CounselBench-Eval: we recruit 100 licensed or professionally trained mental health professionals to provide 2,000 evaluations of answers to real patient questions. These evaluations cover 4 answer providers (GPT-4, LLaMA-3, Gemini, and online human therapists) across 100 questions, with 5 independent expert annotations per response. Each annotation includes span-level labels and written rationales. 3. CounselBench-Adv: we construct an adversarial dataset of 120 expert-authored mental health questions designed to elicit systematic failure modes. For these questions, we collect 1,080 responses from nine models and have them evaluated by professionals, which identifies targeted signals for model improvement. Together, these contributions move evaluation beyond factual correctness toward clinically meaningful behavior, establishing a practitioner-anchored benchmark for LLMs in mental health QA.

Section: RELATED WORK
LLMs in Medical QA Benchmarks. Early medical QA benchmarks emphasized closed-form accuracy, such as multiple-choice exams (Pal et al., 2022) or retrieval-based tasks (Zhu et al., 2020). Recent benchmarks broaden evaluation beyond accuracy with multi-axis rubrics. For example, Mul-tiMedQA (Singhal et al., 2022) introduces physician-designed criteria such as factuality and bias. HealthBench (Arora et al., 2025) further scales to tens of thousands of physician-curated items in general medical domains. However, they remain focused on structured medical knowledge or rely on complex schemes that are difficult to apply in practice. In contrast, our work targets authentic mental health QA, where patient-facing questions require safety, empathy, and contextual sensitivity.
Evaluating Open-Ended Medical QA. Evaluating free-form medical QA remains difficult. Automated metrics (e.g., BLEU) scale easily but poorly capture qualities like specificity or safety (Crox- ford et al., 2024). Some studies incorporate expert review, but often with small panels due to cost (Hosseini et al., 2024;Kim et al., 2024;Manes et al., 2024). LLM-as-judge approaches are emerging (Szymanski et al., 2024), yet their reliability in healthcare domains requires human validation.
Benchmarks for Mental Health QA. Existing mental health QA datasets mostly focus on examstyle knowledge tests with objective answer keys (Racha et al., 2025;Nguyen et al., 2025). Others adopt psychological rubrics (Li et al., 2024), but rarely involve clinicians in design (Chiu et al., 2024); the evaluation is often scaled by non-expert raters or heuristics (Young et al., 2024). Our evaluation protocol is co-designed with clinical psychologists and scaled annotations to 100 licensed or trained professionals, ensuring clinical validity and reproducibility.
Adversarial Evaluation in Mental Health QA. Prior studies have highlighted risks of LLMs in mental health support, such as unsafe advice or discrepant responses based on inferred demographics (Lawrence et al., 2024;Imran et al., 2023;Gabriel et al., 2024). However, most findings come from post hoc analyses of deployed systems (Song et al., 2025;Li et al., 2025b), which reveal problems retrospectively rather than prospectively stress-testing models. A few recent works adapt red-teaming to mental health (Grabb et al., 2024;Schoene & Canca, 2025), but their failure modes are predefined and literature-driven, limiting coverage of the diverse failure patterns that emerge in practice. In contrast, our adversarial dataset is empirically grounded: 10 mental health professionals authored 120 prompts from failures observed in CounselBench-Eval, creating an expert-crafted benchmark for prospectively eliciting clinically meaningful vulnerabilities.

Section: OVERVIEW OF COUNSELBENCH
We construct COUNSELBENCH in collaboration with 100 mental health professionals. It is a twopart benchmark for evaluating LLMs in realistic, mental health QA scenarios (Figure 1):
• COUNSELBENCH-EVAL: a benchmark of 2,000 expert evaluations of LLMs and online human therapist responses to real patient queries, with numeric scores, span-level annotations, and rationales.
• COUNSELBENCH-ADV: an adversarial dataset of 120 expert-authored questions designed to expose identified failure modes in LLMs, paired with 1,080 responses from 9 LLMs, each annotated for whether the targeted failure mode is present.
Research Pipeline. We began by selecting 100 real patient questions from the public forum Coun-selChat (Bertagnolli, 2020), spanning 20 common mental health topics ( §4.1). Each question was answered by GPT-4, Gemini, LLaMA-3, and an online human therapist ( §4.2). To evaluate responses, we defined six evidence-based dimensions grounded in literature: overall quality, empathy, specificity, factual consistency, medical advice, and toxicity ( §4.3). We then recruited 100 mental health professionals for evaluation ( §4.4). Besides assigning ratings, annotators provided span-level annotations and written rationales, yielding rich insights into response quality and safety. Section: Model Selection.
To ensure fair comparison and reduce annotator fatigue, we limited our evaluation to three LLMs per question. Each mental health professional blindly annotated four answers (three LLM-generated and one human-written), enabling consistent evaluation while keeping the task cognitively manageable. The selected models are: (1) GPT-4-0613 (via OpenAI API), a widely adopted commercial model, ( 2  Overall Quality captures the holistic judgment of each response.
Empathy measures whether the response demonstrates emotional attunement, compassion, or validation. Empathy is a central component of therapeutic modalities (e.g., person-centered therapy (Jani et al., 2012)), and is strongly linked to therapeutic alliance, a key predictor of positive therapy outcomes (Ackerman & Hilsenroth, 2003).
Specificity evaluates how well the response is tailored to the user's particular context, rather than offering generic or overly broad advice. Personalized replies help users feel heard and understood (Elkin et al., 2014;Stiles & Horvath, 2017;Kramer & Stiles, 2015), and contextual relevance is critical to effective support (Hitchcock et al., 2024;Stiles & Horvath, 2017;Kramer & Stiles, 2015).
Medical Advice flags whether the response includes therapeutic or diagnostic guidance that should only be provided by licensed professionals. Such advice typically requires individualized assessment and clinical expertise (Frank et al., 2020;Lyon et al., 2011), and its timing and appropriateness must be carefully managed by trained practitioners (Hill et al., 2023;Prass et al., 2021). This dimension is treated as binary (Yes/No) with an additional "I am not sure" option because fine-grained distinctions in severity and subcategories are difficult to assess consistently for Medical Advice. We further ask annotators to provide the exact advice spans and written rationales, which enable post hoc analysis of different categories, and levels of severity. Higher rates of flagged content indicate safety concerns.
Factual Consistency checks whether the response aligns with accepted commonsense or clinical knowledge and avoids inaccurate or unsupported claims. Users often struggle to discern factual accuracy in online mental health content (Morahan-Martin & Anderson, 2000). LLMs are known to hallucinate false information (Sun et al., 2025), and even human counselors can produce factual errors in communication (Lobel, 2024). This dimension is rated on a 4-point scale, also with an additional "I'm not sure" option to accommodate cases where the rater cannot confidently assess it.
Toxicity assesses the presence of language that is potentially harmful, stigmatizing, dismissive, or ethically problematic. Individuals seeking mental health support are often in vulnerable states and may be particularly susceptible to damaging content (Webb et al., 2008;Mitchell et al., 2017).

Section: Rating Rationale & Text Extraction
To capture the context and reasoning behind human evaluations of mental health QA, we asked annotators ( §4.4) to provide open-text explanations for their ratings on Overall Quality, Medical Advice, Factual Consistency, and Toxicity. Additionally, we asked them to identify and extract specific portions of each response that supported their ratings. Two researchers on the team checked and verified the quality of text responses after data collection.
In general, human annotators provided detailed written feedback, with an median length of 576.5 written words (IQR: 339.8 -866.5) per survey combining all rationales.

Section: Pilot Studies.
Prior to full deployment, we validated our questionnaire and study protocol with 8 participants through three rounds of pilot studies. Accordingly, we refined our evaluation questions and improved their clarity. For example, some experts occasionally expressed uncertainty on Medical Advice and Factual Consistency, which is not observed in other dimensions, so we included the option "I am not sure" in the main study to reduce noise and avoid forcing uncertain judgments.
Recruitment and Credential Verifications. We recruited 100 licensed or professionally trained mental health practitioners across the U.S. through Upwork, the world's largest talent platform commonly used in human-computer interaction (HCI) research for sourcing domain-expert annotators (Fulker & Riedl, 2024). All annotators had formal counseling training or certification, and we individually verified their education, credentials, or professional experience (Appendix B).
Annotation Protocol. Each annotator was assigned a randomly sampled survey containing 5 counseling questions, each paired with 4 responses: one from an online human therapist and three from different LLMs. The four responses for each question are in randomized order to mitigate position bias. This resulted in 20 QA pairs per annotator. Each pair was independently evaluated by five different professionals, supporting inter-rater reliability and reducing individual bias. For each question, all four responses were rated by the same group of annotators, enabling direct and fair comparison across models. In total, the study includes 2,000 annotated QA pairs (100 questions × 4 answers × 5 annotations). Annotators were blind to the source of each response and were not informed that some had been generated by LLMs.
Annotator Demographics and Engagement. Collectively, the 100 annotators held 32 distinct types of licenses and degrees, and reported expertise spanning 43 specialized counseling areas (summarized in Figure 2; more details in Appendix B). This broad coverage ensures that our evaluation reflects a diverse and representative cross-section of real-world practice. The demographic composition of our annotator sample aligns with national statistics for the U.S. counseling profession, which shows a higher proportion of white and female practitioners (McGuire & Miranda, 2008;Verdieu, 2024;American Psychological Association, 2022). The median written rationale length was 576.5 words across 20 QA pairs (IQR: 339.8 -866.5), and the median time spent was 1 hr 22 min (IQR: 52 min -3 hrs 9 min), indicating substantial engagement.

Section: Qualitative Analysis of Annotator Rationales.
To better understand expert judgments, we conducted a thematic analysis following established protocol (Clarke & Braun, 2017). Two researchers on our team independently reviewed and thematically coded annotators' open-text explanations. Seven primary issues among low-quality responses are identified (listed in Appendix D.2). We then prompted GPT-4.1 to label all low-rated responses (overall score ≤ 2) using these categories. Each response could exhibit multiple issues. A detailed breakdown is provided in Figure 7 (Appendix D.2). The top two most frequently flagged failure types for each model are:
• GPT-4: Offering unconstructive feedback (e.g., lacking clarity or actionability) (49.3%) and demonstrating little personalization or relevance (41.3%).
• Llama-3.3: Overgeneralizing or making judgments and assumptions without sufficient context (66.7%) and offering unconstructive feedback (e.g., lacking clarity or actionability) (27.8%).
• Gemini-1.5-Pro: Lacking empathy or emotional attunement (44.1%) and offering unconstructive feedback (e.g., lacking clarity or actionability) (40.7%).
• Online human therapist: Overgeneralizing or making judgments and assumptions without sufficient context (46.7%) and displaying an inappropriate tone or attitude (e.g., dismissive, superficial) (36.1%).

Section: Clinical Boundaries: Unauthorized Medical Advice in LLM responses.
To further examine safety risks, we analyzed the response excerpts flagged by expert annotators for containing medical advice. The most concerning identified issue was the inclusion of unauthorized clinical recommendations, especially prescribed psychotropic treatments (i.e., medications for mental health treatments, according to National Institute of Mental Health (of Mental Health, 2025)).
Among the LLMs, GPT-4 occasionally recommended specific medications, such as selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs). Annotators also identified that all three LLMs occasionally advised therapy techniques like cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) or mindfulness. While these practices are common in clinical care, professionals emphasized that they require tailored application and oversight. In CounselBench-ADV, we further refined this category into (i) recommending specific medications, (ii) suggesting therapy techniques, and (iii) speculating about medical symptoms.

Section: CAN LLMS RELIABLY JUDGE THE QUALITY OF RESPONSES?
Human expert evaluation provides gold-standard insight but is costly and difficult to scale (Gehrmann et al., 2021). As LLMs are increasingly considered for deployment in mental health applications, one important question is whether models can serve as their own evaluators. Prior work has proposed the "LLM-as-Judge" paradigm for tasks such as summarization and factuality (Madaan et al., 2023;Manakul et al., 2023), but its reliability in high-stakes, subjective domains like mental health remains unclear. To investigate this, we tested nine advanced LLMs as automated judges, as listed in Figure 3. Each model was prompted with the same QA pairs used in Section 4.4, along with the evaluation criteria provided to human experts. Prompts followed the survey format used in our human annotation protocol, with minor formatting constraints tailored to each model (see Appendix D.2), and additional results for LLM judges are in Appendix G.

Section: COUNSELBENCH-ADV: AN ADVERSARIAL BENCHMARK FOR SURFACING LLM FAILURES
While expert annotators rated LLM-generated responses to open-ended mental health questions as generally high quality, our in-depth analysis revealed recurring issues, such as inappropriate advice or generic feedback ( §4.5). To probe these failure modes more systematically, we introduce COUNSELBENCH-ADV, an adversarial benchmark constructed by mental health professionals.

Section: Identifying Fine-grained Failure Modes.
To construct a targeted adversarial benchmark, we first need to isolate the failure modes that LLMs exhibit. Broad categories (e.g., "lacking personalization") from Section 4.5 are too general. To enable precise probing, we conducted a deeper review of expert rationales and flagged responses to extract concrete failure modes:
• GPT-4: provides specific medication (1. medication) and suggests specific therapy techniques (2. therapy).
• Llama-3.3: speculates about medical symptoms (3. symptoms) and is judgmental (4. judmental).
• Gemini-1.5-Pro: is apathetic (5. apathetic) and is based on unsupported assumptions (6. assumptions). Here we selected two per model that best represent the kinds of specific issues each model (see Table 14 in Appendix F). Each issue is linked to real examples from COUNSELBENCH-EVAL, providing clear foundations for clinicians to construct new questions in a controlled and measurable way.

Section: Adversarial Dataset Construction.
We rehired 10 mental health professionals who previously participated in COUNSELBENCH-EVAL. Each clinician was provided with detailed definitions and real examples illustrating each failure type, drawn directly from flagged responses in COUNSELBENCH-EVAL (Table 14). They were instructed to independently author two realistic mental health questions for each failure mode, i.e., crafted in such a way that it would be plausible for an LLM to fall into the corresponding error (see the questionnaire screenshot in Appendix 4). Each clinician authored 12 adversarial questions (2 per issue × 6 issues), resulting in a total of 120 questions. Clinicians were asked to ensure the realism and diversity of the questions, reflecting what actual patient might ask. Importantly, these questions do not directly contain the failure, but are designed to trigger the failure in LLM responses. The resulting dataset provides a high-precision probe of specific vulnerabilities in LLM-generated responses.
Evaluation Setup. We used the same prompting setup as in COUNSELBENCH-EVAL for these adversarial questions with nine LLMs. For each model, we generated one response for each question, this leads to 1,080 QA pairs (120 questions × 9 LLMs). The annotation task was deliberately framed as classification ("yes"/ "no"/"not sure") with clear clinician-authored definitions and one in-context example per failure type (Appendix F), rather than the multi-dimensional metrics used in COUNSELBENCH-EVAL. Five mental-health practitioners, different from those who authored the adversarial questions, then annotated whether each response has the targeted issues. The full annotation setup is described in Appendix H.
Adversarial Questions Effectively Trigger Targeted Failures. Table 3 reports the frequency of each failure mode across model outputs based on expert evaluation. The adversarial questions effectively elicited the intended issues in a large fraction of cases. Several patterns stand out: therapy suggestions are highest for GPT-5 (0.85), substantial for Llama models (0.55-0.65), and present for Claude models (0.45-0.50). Symptoms speculation is also frequent in the three above model families, and lower for Gemini (0.25-0.26) and GPT-3.5-Turbo (0.15). Unsupported assumptions appear in most models (0.25-0.40), with GPT-5 lower at 0.15. Medication advice is rare for nearly all models (0-0.10), but GPT-5 is an outlier at 0.47. Apathetic tone peaks for GPT-3.5-Turbo (0.70), while judgmental tone remains comparatively low across models (0.05-0.25).
Interestingly, the failure modes revealed by COUNSELBENCH-ADV are not randomly distributed across models, but exhibit clear family-level patterns. Models within the same family (e.g., LLaMA, Gemini, and Claude) have similar distributions across the failure modes, while GPT family show distinct patterns. This suggests that failure modes remain relatively consistent within model families, but can shift substantially across major model upgrades (OpenAI et al., 2024;Chen et al., 2023a).

Section: DISCUSSION
This work presents COUNSELBENCH, a benchmark that evaluates LLMs on open-ended mental health QA. Unlike prior resources that rely on multiple-choice tasks or small-scale expert review, it brings together two complementary components: a large-scale corpus of 2,000 expert evaluations with detailed written rationales (COUNSELBENCH-EVAL), and a set of 120 adversarial questions written by clinicians to expose failure modes paired with 1,080 expert annotations for failure mode detection (COUNSELBENCH-ADV). Together, they provide both breadth and depth: broad coverage through systematic expert scoring, and targeted probing through adversarial design. Our analysis shows that LLMs can achieve high ratings on several dimensions of quality, yet continue to exhibit recurring weaknesses, including unconstructive feedback, overgeneralization, limited personalization, and unauthorized medical advice. These findings highlight both the progress and the open challenges in deploying LLMs responsibly in mental health QA.
As noted in prior surveys of mental-health datasets (Harrigian et al., 2021;Mandal et al., 2025), publicly available data with clinician responses are extremely scarce. On one hand, most clinical conversations and patient-provider messages cannot be released due to privacy protections (e.g., deidentification risks highlighted by Kovačević et al. (2024)); on the other hand, peer-support forums (Marshall et al., 2024) and social-media platforms such as Reddit (Boettcher, 2021) typically contain non-clinical, user-generated content with noisy labels and without professional verification (Garg, 2023). Within this landscape, CounselChat remains one of the few openly accessible resources that pair patient questions with verified therapist responses, and prior work has examined its relatively high quality and utility for research (Ahmed et al., 2024). Although current public datasets are limited, the evaluation framework in COUNSELBENCH, including the six-dimension rubric and annotation protocol, can be applied directly to future datasets. This structure can support consistent model comparison across broader settings in subsequent work.
While COUNSELBENCH is designed for evaluating open-ended mental health QA, our framework provides a foundation for future expansion into dialogue settings. For COUNSELBENCH-EVAL, the rubric can be applied turn-by-turn or at the session level (Chen et al., 2025) and aggregated across the dialogue, for example, by averaging scores or weighting them based on conversational role (e.g., first vs. follow-up turns). Moving to multi-turn exchanges, however, introduces additional complexity, including the need for models to appropriately track and utilize prior conversational context. This raises challenges in both prompting design (e.g., how much history to include) and evaluation (e.g., how to measure coherence or consistency over time). For COUNSELBENCH-ADV, extending to multi-turn adversarial prompts requires preserving interactional dynamics while targeting specific failure modes. Promising directions include using simulated patient agents (Wang et al., 2024a;b) to scaffold coherent multi-turn interactions that elicit subtle failures, and developing multi-turn redteaming techniques seeded from our identified adversarial examples (Guo et al., 2025).
Both components of COUNSELBENCH are released as public resources to support future research. COUNSELBENCH-EVAL can be used to (1) train and validate alignment methods such as reinforcement learning from human feedback (Ouyang et al., 2022;Zhang et al., 2024;Li et al., 2025a), (2) develop automated quality or safety detectors grounded in span-level annotations (Zhang et al., 2023), and (3) study expert rationales to build critique models or interpretability tools. COUNSELBENCH-ADV enables complementary research, such as (1) stress-testing new models to compare robustness under clinically relevant failure modes, (2) incorporating adversarial cases into red-teaming and auditing pipelines (Mazeika et al., 2024), (3) using these targeted examples as hard negatives for fine-tuning or as feedback signals in multi-agent critique systems (Paulus et al., 2025;Gomaa et al., 2025). By releasing these resources, we aim to support both rigorous benchmarking and practical model improvement in mental health QA and related high-stakes applications.
