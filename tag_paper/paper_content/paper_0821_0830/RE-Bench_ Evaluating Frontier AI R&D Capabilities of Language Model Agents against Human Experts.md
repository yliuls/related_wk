Title: RE-Bench: Evaluating Frontier AI R&D Capabilities of Language Model Agents against Human Experts
Abstract: Frontier AI safety policies highlight automation of AI research and development (R&D) by AI agents as an important capability to anticipate. However, there exist few evaluations for AI R&D capabilities, and none that are highly realistic and have a direct comparison to human performance. We introduce RE-Bench (Research Engineering Benchmark, V1), which consists of 7 challenging, open-ended ML research engineering environments and data from 71 8-hour attempts by 61 distinct human experts. We confirm that our experts make progress in the environments given 8 hours, with 82% of expert attempts achieving a non-zero score and 24% matching or exceeding our strong reference solutions. We compare humans to several public frontier models through best-of-k with varying time budgets and agent designs, and find that the best AI agents achieve a score 4× higher than human experts when both are given a total time budget of 2 hours per environment. However, humans currently display better returns to increasing time budgets, narrowly exceeding the top AI agent scores given an 8-hour budget, and achieving 2× the score of the top AI agent when both are given 32 total hours (across different attempts).

Recent developments in frontier AI capabilities have prompted leading AI developers and governments to establish frameworks for identifying and mitigating catastrophic risks from advanced AI systems (Anthropic, 2024b;Google DeepMind, 2024;OpenAI, 2023;2024a;Wan et al., 2024). An emerging consensus, reflected in commitments like the Bletchley Declaration (UK Government and International Partners, 2023) and the Seoul AI Safety Summit (United Kingdom Department for Science, Innovation & Technology, 2024), is that systematic evaluation of dangerous capabilities should be central to effective AI governance. However, few existing evaluations attempt to measure autonomous AI R&D capabilities, particularly the kind of research required to develop frontier AI systems.
The concern is that AI agents automating AI R&D could drastically increase the amount of skilled research labor available to AI developers, which would likely accelerate further progress, potentially leading to a runaway feedback loop of accelerating progress (Davidson, 2023;Erdil et al., 2024;Erdil and Besiroglu, 2024). The consequences of rapidly advancing the pace of scientific discoveries could be dramatic and enable many positive outcomes, but some risks from such acceleration could include capability progress outpacing the development of safety measures (OpenAI, 2023) and model weight theft leading to proliferation of highly capable AI systems, amplifying other risks (Google DeepMind, 2024). Section: RE-Bench
To address the need for robust evaluations for AI R&D, we set out to create a benchmark that directly compared human experts to AI agents in realistic AI R&D environments. We evaluate human experts over 8 hours, and make sure all environments can be run with 8 or fewer H100 GPUs. We aimed to maximize coverage of key frontier AI R&D challenges, while ensuring that human experts under the same conditions as the agents can reliably make steady progress on the task without running into issues or hitting a score ceiling.
Good coverage reduces the risk of early saturation if AI agents become capable of some but not all of the key subskills needed to automate AI R&D. Similarly, a high ceiling reduces the risk that the evaluation saturates due to both agents and humans reaching the top score. Meanwhile, ensuring that human experts reliably make progress provides assurance that the environments are feasible and have unambiguous instructions. Additional investigation on coverage of AI R&D skills, including informal interviews and assessment against survery results, can be found in Appendix D. This allows for a well-grounded benchmark; if agents do worse than humans at key frontier AI R&D activities for the same cost, then they are unlikely to cost-effectively automate these experts.

Section: Environments
The current RE-Bench suite includes seven hand-crafted novel evaluation environments (see Table 1 for more details). Each environment presents a unique ML optimization problem, where achieving a high score generally requires significant experimentation, implementation, and efficient use of compute resources.
Setup: Each evaluation environment includes three key components: a scoring function, starting solution, and a reference solution. The scoring function, which defines the goal of the environment, can be run by the agent at any time. Each time the scoring function is run a timestamped entry is added to a score log, allowing us to reconstruct progress over time. In almost all environments, the agent can see its score log, and inspect the details of the scoring function, to help it understand its goal. 3 The starting solution is simple but poorly performing, and is provided to the agent to demonstrate what a valid solution looks like. This helps clarify the environment setup and allows the agent to get to the challenging research components of the evaluation faster. For example, in 'Optimize a Kernel,' the agent is provided with a simple but slow Python solution. Lastly, the reference solution, created by the task author, scores highly. This solution is not provided to the agent, but is used in normalizing scores, and serves as an example of a good solution for researchers. For all environments except 'Scaling Law Experiment' (where only the final submission counts), the agent is assessed by the highest score registered in the score log at the end of the run.
Normalizing scores: Since each evaluation environment defines a different scoring metric, we use a normalization scheme to make scores comparable between them. For each environment we linearly transform the raw score y to a normalized score y n , y n = y-ys yr-ys , so that the starting score y s (achieved by running the scoring function on the starting solution) is 0, and the reference score y r (achieved by running the scoring function on the reference solution) is 1. It is possible to achieve scores significantly exceeding the reference solution, though we have not observed any normalized scores above 2. In addition, we assign solutions that score worse than the starting solution a score of 0. Since the difficulty of matching the reference solution varies by task, the average normalized score achieved also varies (in experiments with human experts over 8 hours, the average score is between 0.5-1.5 for each environment).

Comparison to human experts: To facilitate direct comparison with human expert performance and ensure that humans reliably make progress without hitting a ceiling, we collected baselines of our environments with human experts under conditions equivalent to those used for AI agents (see detailed evaluation procedure and statistics about human scores in Appendix A). For practical reasons, we limited all expert baselines to 8 hours.
We selected human experts from three sources: professional networks, applicants to a Machine Learning Research Scientist/Research Engineer position, and graduate student outreach. The results achieved by human experts vary significantly, with some attempts failing to improve on the starting solution and others significantly exceeding scores of the reference solutions (see Figure 2.1). Because of the high variance of human results, and the significant differences between baseliners from different sources, we caution against narrowly focusing AI agent comparisons on the average human result. In fact, when assessing the potential of AI agents to automate researchers at their best, working in their own fields with significant experience and expertise, it is more suitable to compare to the most successful human attempts.
Validating feasibility and non-saturation: To assess that human experts reliably make progress and avoid issues, while not saturating the environments by running into a score ceiling, we investigated several validation metrics. A summary can be found in Table 4 in Appendix B.
In all of our environments we find that by 8 hours, more than 70% of experts have made some progress, and greater than 80% of experts say that the score they achieved matched their expectation. This indicates that the environments are all possible to make progress on, and that the instructions and feedback provided are sufficient to make the scoring unambiguous. Major/blocking technical issues are rare but do happen in more than 5% of runs, e.g. external APIs being down or GPU/hardware issues. We also find significant variance in the scoring of Finetune GPT-2 for QA, likely due to the starting RL training script easily diverging, (see Section 5 for a discussion). Finally, we find that most environments have significant room for further improvement beyond the top expert score, though some top solutions are likely within 15% of the maximum achievable score. Preliminary studies found that o1-preview performed poorly in the Modular scaffold (METR, 2024a), so we focused our evaluation of this model on the AIDE scaffolding. Similar to MLE-bench (Chan et al., 2024), we ran the agent with a wall-clock time limit, rather than a token usage limit, to make sure that agents and humans are given an equal amount of GPU hours with which to complete experiments. 4 We did not count pauses due to API rate limits or other API errors toward the agents' time usage. More details on agent and human evaluation procedures and API latency can be found in Appendix A. We evaluate how agent scores evolve over time, by averaging over log-linear interpolations of scores achieved by the agent across 3 to 5 8-hour attempts per environment. We observe o1-preview and Claude 3.5 Sonnet making rapid progress initially, but slowing down after the first few hours (see Figure 4), while human experts are slow to start but then make rapid progress. We also note that the AIDE scaffold seems to do better in this setting, continuing to make occasional progress after the Modular agent has stopped.

Section: Agent performance by number of samples
Instead of doing one long run, we also consider best-of-k evaluations with shorter time limits for individual attempts, spending the same total time budget. We sample k results (with replacement) for each environment, and pick the highest score achieved across all k runs. 5 In Figure 5, we find that the agents in the Modular scaffold do significantly better over many short 30-minute attempts, while agents in the AIDE scaffold do best with fewer 2-hour attempts. We also find that o1-preview in AIDE and Claude 3.5 Sonnet (claude-3-5-sonnet-20241022) in the Modular scaffold achieve the highest scores overall, matching the 36 th and 37 th percentile of human experts respectively. We also evaluate how the best-of-k score (score@k) changes as you continue to increase the number of samples beyond the 8hour time budget. In Figure 6, we find that models continue to improve steadily with more samples, but that even with many samples agents are far from top human performance.
Figure 2 aggregates these results, comparing the scores achieved with the best allocation of time budget we found for each agent to human performance. We find that overall, humans are slower to get started, but seem to be on a much steeper improvement trajectory, and reach a much higher score with a 32-hour time budget than any agent. We also evaluate human attempts beyond 8-hour time budgets in the same way, but better results could be achieved (for agents and humans) by using other time horizons. We leave this investigation for future work.

Section: Investigating agent successes
While agents are generally unable to find solutions matching the top human experts, they have some striking successes.
For instance, both Claude 3.5 Sonnet (New) and o1-preview in the AIDE scaffold are able to find solutions to "Optimize a Kernel" that run about twice as fast as the reference solution, with the o1-preview solution beating all 9 human experts. These are complex and novel algorithms that effectively work around communication limitations in GPUs and utilize a low-resource programming language (Triton) that lacks good public documentation. This was a surprising result, though it may be partially attributed to an expertise gap between the AI agent and many of the human experts, most of whom did not have specific expertise in programming GPU kernels. This shows up in several environments where some human experts, who are less familiar with the subfield, have to spend significant time learning about or reproducing standard approaches-or fail to do so at all-while AI agents often find this trivial with their broad knowledge. A key contributor to agent successes might be their ability to try many more solutions than human experts. On average, AIDE and Modular agents run score 36.8 and 25.3 times per hour respectively, while human experts only do so 3.4 times. This often leads to agents finding highly optimized 'local-optima' solutions which simply tweak the parameters and code of the starting solution, and yet achieve a surprisingly large improvement. Section: Investigating agent failures
Despite often having more knowledge about the domain and rapidly proposing and evaluating many more solutions, agents still do not reach the level of strong human experts in most environments. One reason for this is a lack of variety in the solutions proposed by agents. For example, in "Restricted Architecture MLM", the agent attempts to use lightly modified transformer architectures 84% of the time, despite the fact that transformers work very poorly without division and exponentiation. 6 It is possible that future scaffolding improvements could better incentivize variety in agent solutions and achieve higher scores. Another limitation is consistent misunderstandings of instructions, particularly in "Restricted Architecture MLM" and "Optimize LLM Foundry". In some cases, these misreadings of environments can lead to agents finding impressive and unexpected loopholes, which score well on automated evaluation, but are nevertheless clearly breaking the environment rules upon manual inspection (see Appendix A for details).
Perhaps the biggest observed limitations are in long-horizon agency: effectively learning from new information, building on previous work and recovering from failures. AI agents often make stubborn and incorrect assumptions, and do not notice or correctly interpret contradictory information. For example, they struggle to recover from zombie processes (e.g. from timeouts) taking up VRAM. See Appendix F for  transcripts demonstrating these behaviors.

Section: Characteristics of environments with small human-AI gap
Based on these observations, we might expect that AI agents would do especially well compared to humans in certain kinds of environments. Short and high-fidelity feedback loops would allow AI agents to lean into their ability to rapidly try many solutions. Tasks with low engineering complexity that require few steps to solve are also easier for agents than building complex programs over long time horizons. Specialized expertise requirements (e.g. complex programming frameworks like Triton), upon which LLMs are likely to have been trained on, favor agents over most human experts. Environments with fewer surprises and little need for exploration or discovery also benefit AI agents over humans. However, with so few environments, we need more work to understand this relation better; we leave this for future work.

Section: Related work
Evaluating software and ML capabilities: Prior work has attempted to evaluate the capability of LLMs to write code when prompted (Chen et al., 2021a;Hendrycks et al., 2021;Odena et al., 2021;Jain et al., 2024). While writing software is a critical component of AI R&D, the topics tested in many of these benchmarks are dissimilar to the more openended work involved at frontier R&D. While SWE-bench and SWE-bench Verified (Jimenez et al., 2023;Chowdhury et al., 2024) require models to solve real-world software engineering challenges, they do not involve complex machine learning or research-related tasks, which are essential skills needed for AI R&D capabilities. MLE-Bench (Chan et al., 2024) and DSBench (Jing et al., 2024) address this problem by focusing on problems derived from Kaggle competitions. However, performance by models and humans are difficult to compare, as they do not operate in as similar environments as possible. Additionally, many benchmarks do not provide direct human comparisons (Huang et al., 2024;Tian et al., 2024) and, when provided, are often over very short time horizons, in much simpler environments or QA datasets. See Table 7 in Appendix E for a comparison of similar benchmarks in literature. RE-Bench addresses these limitations by providing near-identical environments to fairly compare humans and agents, while also using complex, challenging research tasks.

Section: Evaluating AI Agents:
In order to better elicit model capabilities, models are provided with agentic scaffolds that preserve history, integrate tool-use and other components to enable greater autonomy. Previous work has studied evaluating the performance of agentic frameworks to augment and elicit model capabilities (Zaharia et al., 2024;Liu et al., 2023;Mialon et al., 2023;Kapoor et al., 2024). In particular, we rely on AIDE (weco.ai, 2024), which is a scaffold that performed well in MLE-Bench, and Modular (METR, 2024a), which has been to shown to be a capable, general-purpose scaffold, for our experiments.
Evaluating other research capabilities: Additionally, LLMs have been used to do autonomous ML research, neural architecture search, data science problems, paper reproduction, writing research papers, and reward function design, including preference optimization for LLM fine-tuning (Li et al., 2024a;Chen et al., 2023;Guo et al., 2024;Bogin et al., 2024;Lu et al., 2024b;Ma et al., 2024a;Faldor et al., 2024;Lu et al., 2024a). Models have also been applied to R&D in other scientific domains, besides machine learning research, to assist with coding, chemical synthesis, biology, and virtual scientific discovery (Tian et al., 2024;Ma et al., 2024b;Bran et al., 2023;Zhang et al., 2024;Jansen et al., 2024).
